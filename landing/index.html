TerraClicker Redux

Terraform the cosmos one click at a time. Mostly harmless.

You have a planet, a quota, and no particular say in either. Click it, industrialise it, and watch the ice caps retreat, the oceans find the basins and city lights wake on the night side — live, in the shader, not on a progress bar. Finish it and it doesn't disappear: it takes an orbit and stays there while the next, stranger world drifts in. Later, when you have a ship, you can go back and land on it.

The loop

An incremental game with the usual bones — click, buy, multiply, come back tomorrow — and one difference: the thing your numbers are doing is visible from orbit.

  1. 01 Click the planet
  2. 02 Earn Terraform Units
  3. 03 Buy 14 tiers of industry
  4. 04 Fill four gauges
  5. 05 Finish the world — keep it
  6. ↻ A stranger world arrives

Four gauges, one bottleneck

Every building feeds one or two of them. A world is finished when all four hit target — and each new world moves the targets, so the build that just worked stops working.

  • Thermal volcanic worlds arrive nearly full
  • Atmospheric thickens the limb, softens the terminator
  • Hydrologic raises the sea until it finds the basins
  • Biotic green climbs out of the coastlines last

Features

The planet is the interface

Your numbers are the picture

Thermal, Atmospheric, Hydrologic and Biotic aren't a readout of progress — they are uniforms feeding a node-material shader. Warm the world and the caps retreat. Raise the water and it pools where the terrain is low. Push Biotic and vegetation climbs out of the coastlines while lights come on across the night side.

There is no separate artwork for "sixty percent done". There's just the planet.

Five worlds make a system

Nothing you finish is thrown away

Complete a commission and the world doesn't vanish to make room for the next one. It takes an orbit and stays. Scroll out and there's your whole career at once: worlds you terraformed circling the stars they were billed to, freighters running the routes between them, five systems folding into a galaxy that starts, slowly, to turn.

Any of it can be clicked. The camera leaves the rail, flies over, and rebuilds those worlds from the seeds they were terraformed with — the same planets, not a diagram.

Take the helm

The camera is also a ship

Press F and the crane becomes the company runabout: first person, hold-to-steer, W to thrust, shift for an improbability boost. Nothing is staged for you and nothing is scenery — worlds, moons and stars are all solid, and a proximity governor parks you on the hull line instead of letting you sail through a planet.

Speed near a surface is capped by how fast the ground sweeps across the glass, so a low pass is a crawl and getting around a world takes half a minute. That one rule is the difference between a marble and a place.

Groundfall

And then you can land on it

Point the nose at any world with a floor and hold the dive. The runabout flies the entry itself — plasma sheath, cloud deck, a glide onto a shelf the autoland picks — and you get out and walk around on a planet you made, at one unit to the metre.

It is the same world. The surface evaluates the identical continent field the orbital shader does, so the coastline you watched fill from a hundred thousand kilometres up is the coastline under your boots, and a test holds the two together. Ore seams live on a lattice fixed to the planet rather than to your approach, which means the ground remembers: a seam you work is worked for good, and a claim you stake is still standing the next time you come down, on a world you may not visit again for hours.

Weather, and the people in it

The lights are somebody's windows

Weather is arithmetic — a function of the world, the spot and the clock, with no stored state — so both scales agree about it: the front you can see turning over a planet from orbit is the dust you land in. It never damages you. It changes what you can do. Dust chokes the scanner and uncovers seams the sand was sitting on; whiteouts erase your instruments and leave heat, so getting back to the ship becomes navigation; electrical storms feed the scanner instead.

And the settlement lights you saw on the night side turn out to be somewhere. Walk into one and it's the town those exact lights described — lit by how well you've looked after the place, with the hardware from the delivery paperwork standing in the yard, scaffolding up wherever somebody's request is still open, and whatever lives on the world going about its business nearby. Look after a world and its windows are lit when you visit. Ignore it and they go out, a few at a time, in the same places.

The Deep Field

Not everything out there is yours

Everything else in the sky is a monument to something you finished. Fifteen things are not. They're placed once, by the master seed alone, and never move — a generation ship still going, a teapot, a restaurant that politely declines to be reached. No two players get the same sky.

At range they're an anonymous glint worth a detour. Hold E to scan; hold it again up close to board. Salvage buys ship refits and only ship refits: the idle economy never touches it, so if you never leave the planet you lose nothing but the view. Your logbook survives prestige. It was never part of the portfolio.

Prestige

Then Magrathea calls

Sooner or later a custom-planet firm with an old reputation and a long lunch break offers to buy the entire portfolio. Take the money. Everything resets except the Blueprints, which buy permanent advantages from a catalogue — and a short list of things the mice were never buying: your Guide entries, your logbook, the laws you passed, the monuments.

The second run is faster, wider and considerably stranger.

Also in the box

Marvin

Buy him once. He clicks the planet once a second, forever, and mentions how he feels about it. Brain the size of a planet, employed to click one.

Vogon poetry readings

A fleet arrives, moors above the work site, and reads. Production halves until it's finished. Research a Babel Fish and it halves the damage instead of the reading.

Improbability

Heart of Gold drives raise the improbability level, and improbable things start turning up: whales, petunias, refractive bubbles that are worth clicking on.

Research, including the Answer

A queue you start and walk away from. One item takes 42 real hours and pays a permanent +42% to everything, through every prestige. Its description is "42". The follow-up never finishes.

While you were hitchhiking

Idle games are about coming back, so offline progress is a feature and not a patch. You return to a report on what happened, at up to full efficiency once researched.

Six planet types, fourteen quirks

A volcanic world arrives with Thermal mostly filled and triple the Hydrologic target, so your dusty seeders are suddenly the bottleneck. One world refuses to terraform on Mondays. One has a pet asteroid, which has a name you are not told. Planet 42 is always Earth, and its quirk does nothing whatsoever.

The Survey Skimmer

A ground-effect sled that folds out of the runabout, bought with salvage in three ranks: one gets you off your feet, two puts the scanner on a mast steady enough to work in weather, three makes open water something you cross rather than something you stop at. Park it anywhere and it stays parked, on the compass, warm enough to find in a whiteout.

Orbital surveys

Every arrival opens with a pick-one-of-three report — rich geothermal seams, an improbability shadow, paperwork lost in transit. Ten seconds of agency before the grind resumes.

The Total Perspective Vortex

One continuous zoom from the planet under your cursor out to the cosmic web, with a small arrow and a short label. You are here.

An actual ending

There is a booking at a restaurant at the end of things. It turns out to have been made already. It pays no multiplier — the reward for finishing is that you finished.

Under the hood

Deterministic engine
250 ms ticks, seeded RNG streams stored in the save. One eight-hour offline catch-up and 480 one-minute ticks produce bit-identical states. That's a unit test, not a hope.
WebGPU renderer
three.js WebGPURenderer with TSL node materials driving the planet from the four gauges, automatic WebGL2 fallback, and device tiers so phones get a scene they can actually run.
Saves you own
Schema-validated, versioned and migrated, held in localStorage with rolling backups, and exportable as a compressed string you can carry to another browser.
No server
No accounts, no telemetry, no ads, nothing phoning home. The entire game is a static bundle served off GitHub Pages, and it keeps running when the network doesn't.

Questions the Guide anticipates

Is it really free?

Yes. No account, no ads, no purchases, no premium currency, nothing to unsubscribe from.

Where are my saves?

In your browser's localStorage, with rolling backups, saved continuously. Clearing site data for this domain will clear the game, so if you care about a run, export the save string from Settings — it's also how you move a run to another browser.

Does it work on a phone?

Yes, with a one-hand portrait layout and touch controls for flight. The renderer uses WebGPU where the device has it and falls back to WebGL2 where it doesn't, dropping scene detail by device tier.

Do I have to leave the tab open?

No. You'll be caught up when you come back, with a report on what happened while you were away. Research raises both the offline efficiency and the cap.

Do I have to fly the ship or land on anything?

No. Flight, the Deep Field and groundfall are a sealed sideline: they pay salvage, salvage buys ship equipment, and nothing out there ever touches the idle economy. If you never leave the planet you lose nothing but the view. It's there for the days you want to go and look at something.

How long is it?

First prestige lands somewhere in the first hour or two. After that it's an idle game — it's as long as you want, and it has a genuine ending for people who want one.

Is it finished?

It's feature-complete against its design document and it's playable end to end. Balance across the later systems is still moving, and it's a solo project, so expect the occasional rough edge and the occasional sudden improvement.

Is this an official Hitchhiker's Guide game?

No. It's an unaffiliated, unofficial homage written in its own words — no text from the books is reproduced, and it isn't endorsed by or connected to the rights holders. If you like the register, the books are better and you should read those.

The planet isn't going to click itself

Well — it is, eventually. You have to buy Marvin first.
